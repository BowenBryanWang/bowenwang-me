Filtering Doesn't Change Your Mix. Fixing the Mix Does.

Every multi-environment RL run contains three mixtures: the prompts you dispatch, the groups that survive filtering, and the gradient that actually moves the weights. Only the third is your objective. We show that zero-advantage filtering leaves it alone, that holding the post-filter mix distorts it by one over the acceptance rate, and that per-sequence loss normalization reweights environments by inverse length and keeps re-mixing them as episodes grow. One ledger rule fixes both; one log statistic tells you whether you need it.

Bowen WangDraft · pre-registration · no results yet

Frontier agentic models are now trained on hundreds to thousands of environments in one run. DeepSeek-V3.2 synthesized 1,827 agent environments[7]; LongCat-Flash-Thinking-2601 trains on more than ten thousand[6]. Labs disagree on whether to train them jointly or as specialists that are later distilled, and both camps cite interference between domains as the reason for their choice[29]. Almost every report that trains jointly also describes hand-tuning the mixture[18][19][20], and at least two systems built machinery specifically to hold the mixture fixed while filtering rates and episode durations drift[3][4].

This post asks a narrow question underneath that machinery: when you set environment weights, are those the weights your gradient actually uses? The answer depends on two implementation choices that look unrelated to the mixture: how the batch is refilled after filtering, and how the loss is normalized. We derive the exact environment weights each choice produces, show that two widely used patterns get them wrong in opposite directions, and give a rule that makes the realized weights equal the intended ones by construction.

The claims, in the order we defend them:

  • Filtering is free. Dropping groups whose advantages are all zero, then refilling with the original dispatch weights, leaves every environment’s gradient weight unchanged. It only rescales the step.
  • Fixing the mix is not. Refilling so that the post-filter share matches the target multiplies each environment’s weight by 1/ak1/a_k1/ak​, one over its acceptance rate. Near-solved and near-impossible environments are amplified, and in a two-task construction the expected update becomes exactly zero while the true gradient is not.
  • The normalizer is a mixer. Per-sequence 1/∣o∣1/|o|1/∣o∣ normalization, which GRPO as written, GSPO and several production recipes use, weights environment kkk by roughly qk E[1/Lk]q_k\,\mathbb{E}[1/L_k]qk​E[1/Lk​]. A 40k-token environment dispatched at 30% receives about 2% of the gradient, and less as its episodes grow. Global token-mean does not have this problem.
  • One ledger rule fixes both. Choose dispatch weights for speed, then weight each episode by wk/(qkκk)w_k/(q_k\kappa_k)wk​/(qk​κk​). The objective no longer depends on how you sample.
  • Whether it matters is measurable for free. The realized gradient share per environment can be computed from existing logs. That number decides whether this is a headline or a diagnostic, and we commit to reporting it either way.

No result in this post has been run yet. As in the previous post in this series[23], we write the predictions down before the experiments.

Scope
In scope
  • One policy trained on KKK environments with a GRPO-family estimator: a group-relative baseline, optionally divided by the group standard deviation, binary or bounded rewards.
  • Batch construction after zero-advantage filtering: refill rules, per-environment quotas, oversampling.
  • Loss aggregation: global token-mean, per-sequence 1/∣o∣1/|o|1/∣o∣, per-group (prompt-average) normalizers, and GSPO’s length-normalized sequence ratio.
  • The question: given target weights www, which of these choices make the expected update equal ∑kwk∇Jk\sum_k w_k\nabla J_k∑k​wk​∇Jk​, and by how much do the common ones miss?
  • Deliverables: three exact statements, a ledger rule of roughly fifteen lines, a log statistic, and two controlled experiments.
Out of scope
  • Choosing www. We take the target weights as given. We sketch one principled way to produce them, a constrained objective, only to show that it needs the same estimator fix.
  • Cost-aware allocation. How many rollouts to spend per environment is a variance question with known answers[14]. We use it as a tool, not a claim.
  • Transfer and interference. Measuring which environments help each other online is covered by TAC and HarnessBandit[16][17].
  • Curricula over horizons and tasks. Whether learning-progress samplers confuse budget growth with learning is the next post in this series.
  • Drops that carry gradient. Staleness, timeout and turn-cap drops are not zero-advantage filters; they are the subject of the previous post[23].
  • Optimizer effects. We note where Adam or Muon absorb or compress a weight, and leave the net effect to the experiments.

The same argument at the level of single prompts is folklore for DAPO’s dynamic sampling[1], and ARCUS states it as a corollary[12]. Lifting it to environments is immediate. We state it because the systems in the next section act as if it were false.

The batch share after filtering is qkak/aˉq_k a_k/\bar aqk​ak​/aˉ, not qkq_kqk​. An environment that is nearly solved looks starved. MT-GRPO is the clearest case because it states the objective. Its appendix argues that “enforcing the ratio-preserving constraint is what converts the intended weight into actual gradient contribution”[2]. Result 1 says the opposite: refilling with the original weights already does that, and enforcing the constraint undoes it. In fairness, its hardest task also has the highest filter rate, so the 1/a1/a1/a factor happens to push toward its worst-case goal, and its ablation improves that task. Whether the tilt helps is a separate question from whether it is the one the designer wrote down.

Result 3: the normalizer is a mixer

The second hidden mixer is the loss normalizer. Let each episode receive an aggregation weight h(τ)h(\tau)h(τ): hhh is a constant under global token-mean, 1/L(τ)1/L(\tau)1/L(τ) under per-sequence 1/∣o∣1/|o|1/∣o∣ normalization, and roughly 1/(GLˉx)1/(G\bar L_x)1/(GLˉx​) under a per-group normalizer. Holding the baseline fixed at JxJ_xJx​, the score-function identity gives exactly

E[g^]=∑kqk Ex∼Dk[Hx(θ) ∇Jx+∇ Covx(R,h)],Hx=E[h∣x].\mathbb{E}[\hat g]=\sum_k q_k\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim D_k}\Big[H_x(\theta)\,\nabla J_x+\nabla\,\mathrm{Cov}_x\big(R,h\big)\Big],\qquad H_x=\mathbb{E}[h\mid x].E[g^​]=k∑​qk​Ex∼Dk​​[Hx​(θ)∇Jx​+∇Covx​(R,h)],Hx​=E[h∣x].
Result 3
  • Global token-mean is neutral. Every episode enters with its full score function times one shared scalar, which is sequence-level REINFORCE. Environment weights equal qkq_kqk​. Long environments do not dominate.
  • Per-sequence and per-group normalizers reweight. Environment kkk gets weight ≈qk E[1/Lk](θ)\approx q_k\,\mathbb{E}[1/L_k](\theta)≈qk​E[1/Lk​](θ), which depends on the parameters. The update is then not the gradient of any fixed weighted objective.
  • The covariance term is the familiar within-task length bias[9]: it rewards short successes and long failures.

The drift creates a plausible feedback loop in agentic training. Failures in a hard long-horizon environment already tend to run to the turn cap; the covariance term lengthens them further; the environment’s weight falls; it learns more slowly and fails more. We have not observed this loop. It is a prediction.

Who uses which normalizer. GRPO as written in DeepSeek-V3.2 normalizes per sequence[7]. GSPO’s length-normalized sequence ratio has the same effect on the gradient[8], and LongCat-Flash-Thinking-2601 trains its multi-domain run with GSPO[6]. ScaleRL’s prompt-average[10] and MiniMax’s per-group CISPO normalizer[11] are per-group. verl’s default token-mean and prime-rl’s global denominator are neutral. Two reports already point at aggregation mattering for agents: GLM-4.5 found a token-weighted mean clearly better than a sequence mean[21], and CompactionRL lost 6.8 points on SWE-bench Verified without token-level normalization[26], and AgentRL reports that tasks of different lengths learn at very different rates[25]. None attributes the effect to environment weights.

Doing it right

The three results separate cleanly into what should be chosen and what should be corrected.

1. Write the objective down

Either a weighted objective Jw=∑kwkJkJ_w=\sum_k w_kJ_kJw​=∑k​wk​Jk​, or a constrained one that improves the total without letting any capability fall more than a tolerance[15]:

max⁡θ ∑kwkJk(θ)s.t.Jk(θ) ≥ Jk(θref)−δk.\max_\theta\ \sum_k w_kJ_k(\theta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad J_k(\theta)\ \ge\ J_k(\theta_{\text{ref}})-\delta_k .θmax​ k∑​wk​Jk​(θ)s.t.Jk​(θ) ≥ Jk​(θref​)−δk​.

The constrained version is solved with per-environment multipliers μk≥0\mu_k\ge0μk​≥0 and an update direction ∑k(wk+μk)∇Jk\sum_k (w_k+\mu_k)\nabla J_k∑k​(wk​+μk​)∇Jk​. Here the estimator matters twice. If the batch holds the post-filter share, the multipliers believe they are applying wk+μkw_k+\mu_kwk​+μk​ while the gradient applies (wk+μk)/ak(w_k+\mu_k)/a_k(wk​+μk​)/ak​, and the dual updates chase the wrong target. Any scheme that produces weights, whether a designer, a dual variable or a min-max game, needs an estimator that delivers them.

2. Choose dispatch for speed, freely

Once the objective is fixed, the dispatch distribution qqq only affects variance. Cost-aware rules such as qk∝wkσk/ckq_k\propto w_k\sigma_k/\sqrt{c_k}qk​∝wk​σk​/ck​​ are textbook stratified sampling, and DUET derives the per-prompt version for RLVR[14]; earlier allocators work per prompt and by rollout count[27]. Oversampling a low-acceptance environment is fine. Keep qk≥ϵ wkq_k\ge\epsilon\,w_kqk​≥ϵwk​ so the correction below stays bounded.

3. Weight each episode by the ledger

Give every episode from environment kkk a fixed loss weight

λk=wkqk κk,\lambda_k=\frac{w_k}{q_k\,\kappa_k},λk​=qk​κk​wk​​,

where κk\kappa_kκk​ is the per-episode aggregation factor: one for token-sum with a batch-wide denominator, or a constant measured once at warm-up if a per-sequence normalizer must stay. Then the expected update is ∝∑kwk∇Jk\propto\sum_k w_k\nabla J_k∝∑k​wk​∇Jk​ regardless of qqq, filtering, or length drift. If you oversample to hold the post-filter share, qk∝wk/akq_k\propto w_k/a_kqk​∝wk​/ak​ and the ledger weight becomes λk∝ak\lambda_k\propto a_kλk​∝ak​: keep the extra groups for their lower variance, and multiply them by the acceptance rate.

4. Correct what carries gradient, and nothing else

Zero-advantage filtering should not be corrected. Drops that remove gradient-carrying episodes, such as stale, timed-out or capped rollouts, should be, and leaving them uncorrected is the subsidy described in the previous post[23]. prime-rl’s closed proposal corrected both kinds with one factor[4]; they need opposite treatment.

5. Log the ledger

Per environment and step, log dispatched groups, accepted groups, mean generated tokens, the analytic weight qkλkκkq_k\lambda_k\kappa_kqk​λk​κk​, and the realized gradient share

Boundary conditions
  • Regularizers are not filtered for free. KL and entropy terms are nonzero on groups with zero advantage. Dropping those groups reweights the regularizer by qkakq_ka_kqk​ak​. If per-environment KL strength matters, as DeepSeek-V3.2 reports it does[7], compute it over dispatched groups or apply the ledger to it.
  • The filter must see the final advantage. If reward shaping, such as a length penalty, gives all-pass groups a nonzero advantage, filtering on pass labels drops real gradient and becomes an outcome-dependent mask[23]. MiMo-V2.6 combines shaped rewards with all-pass filtering; the text does not say which signal the filter uses[3].
  • Std normalization adds its own factor. Filtering stays neutral, but dividing by the group std scales each prompt’s expected update by a factor that also grows at extreme pass rates, and it compounds with 1/a1/a1/a.

We expect the most serious objection to be that both new results are short derivations. We agree. The contribution is that the derivations contradict patterns that shipped in production systems and a published method, and that the size of the effect is measurable from logs that already exist.

Experimental plan

Phase 0: the ledger census

Setup. Take any existing multi-environment run. For each environment and step, reconstruct dispatched and accepted groups, mean generated length, and the analytic weight under the run’s refill rule and normalizer. At five checkpoints, compute the realized share sks_ksk​ with per-environment gradient buffers.

Gate. If realized and target shares stay within 1.5× of each other throughout training and do not drift, the experiments below are still run, but the post is rewritten around the diagnostic rather than the distortion.

Experiment A: refill rules

Design. Isolate acceptance from length: two task pools of the same multi-turn tool-use environment, one that saturates during training (starting pass rate near 0.8) and one that stays hard (near 0.3). Lengths are matched by construction. Global token-mean, mean-only advantages, Qwen3-4B, 200 to 300 steps, G = 8.

Arms, three seeds each. (a) No filtering. (b) Filter and refill with the dispatch weights. (c) Filter and hold the post-filter share, capped at 20×. (d) Arm c plus the ledger weight λk∝ak\lambda_k\propto a_kλk​∝ak​. Ablations: G = 16; std-normalized advantages.

Measurements. Realized share sk(t)s_k(t)sk​(t), per-pool success on fixed held-out sets, global step size, per-pool KL, and the fraction of the easy pool’s gradient that comes from groups with a single failure.

Experiment B: normalizers

Design. Isolate length from acceptance: two environments with similar starting pass rates and very different lengths, a short function-calling environment under 1k generated tokens and a multi-turn terminal or SWE-lite environment above 10k tokens with thinking on.

Arms, three seeds each. (a) Global token-mean. (b) Per-sequence normalization. (c) Per-sequence normalization with ledger weights chosen to match arm b at step 0 and then frozen, which isolates the drift from the initial tilt. (d) Optional: GSPO in its default form against its token form with the ledger.

Measurements. Realized share, mean length and success per environment over time; failure-length growth in the long environment.

Predictions and falsifiers
  • P1, the algebra. In Experiment A, arm b’s realized shares match dispatch within seed noise; arm c’s match wk/akw_k/a_kwk​/ak​ and grow as the easy pool saturates; arm d returns to the target.
  • P2, the consequence. At equal steps, arm c improves the hard pool more slowly than arms b and d, and its late updates on the easy pool are dominated by single-failure groups.
  • P3, the normalizer. In Experiment B, arm b’s long-environment share starts near the predicted inverse-length ratio and falls as episodes lengthen; its long-environment success lags arms a and c. Arm c starts at arm b’s share and stays flat.
  • P4, the loop. Failure length in the long environment grows faster under arm b than under arm a.
What would change our mind
  • Realized and target shares differ by less than 1.5× in Phase 0 and in arm c: the distortion is real but too small to matter, and we say so.
  • Arms c and d learn within seed noise of each other despite a measured distortion of 2× or more: the optimizer or shared features wash it out, which is itself worth reporting.
  • Arm c of Experiment B tracks arm b: the drift is harmless and only the initial tilt matters.
Out of scope, and the rest of the series

This post covers the first half of multi-task RL done right: making the gradient implement the objective. The second half is about the signals that choose the objective and the sampler.

  • Budget-matched progress. Learning-progress samplers compare an environment’s reward across steps. When the episode budget grows during training, as it does in Avalon-M1’s schedule from two to more than five hours[28], the signal mixes learning with extra time. Nested task curricula cause the same confusion. That is the next post.
  • Transfer, harnesses and cost. Online transfer estimation and harness scheduling are covered by TAC and HarnessBandit[16][17]; cost-aware allocation by DUET[14]. We cite them rather than compete with them.
  • Earlier in the series. Every Loss Mask Is a Reward covers drops that carry gradient, which are the ones this post tells you to correct.
