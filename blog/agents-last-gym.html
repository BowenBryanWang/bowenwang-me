Agents’ Last Gym

Bowen Wang
Nov 2026
  • Introduction
  • Citation
Contents
  • Introduction
  • Citation

Personal and daily-work tasks are no longer a challenge for agents driven by frontier models. They manage email, fill out forms, and edit documents and spreadsheets. The benchmarks built around this kind of work, navigating websites, operating desktop applications and running simulated office workflows ,,,, were open problems only a few years ago. Frontier models now solve most of them, and training environments for such tasks can be generated at scale ,,.

The work that professionals are paid for is a different matter. Consider retopologizing a sculpted character so that it deforms cleanly at every joint, carrying an electronic subsystem from a requirements sheet to a routed board that passes design-rule check, or rebuilding a researcher’s visualization of a simulation from raw data. Each of these is a project rather than an action. It takes a trained expert hours, it rests on a chain of interdependent decisions in specialized software, and its deliverable either passes the profession’s own checks or it does not. On such work, frontier agents remain far from reliable. On the hardest tier of Agents’ Last Exam the average full pass rate is below 1% . The best agent fully automates 2.5% of the real freelance projects in the Remote Labor Index , finishes 15 of 56 professional video-editing jobs on Timeline-Bench , and scores 4.58% on functional circuit-board construction .

Closing this gap requires training tasks of the same caliber, and such tasks are scarce and costly to produce. This is not for lack of data. Repositories have been mined into large collections of verifiable software-engineering tasks ,,, computer-use pipelines synthesize tasks for hundreds of desktop applications ,, and human demonstrations are collected and mined from tutorials and videos ,,. But synthesized tasks rarely reach beyond what their synthesizer can already do, so they cluster at the level of daily work, and expert-level tasks written by hand cost hours of a specialist’s time each. Agents’ Last Exam drew on more than 250 industry experts to assemble about 1.5K tasks, held out for evaluation . Professional work now has its exam. It does not have a gym.

We introduce Agents’ Last Gym (ALG), a large-scale suite of expert-level professional tasks built through reverse synthesis from real, finished work. Rather than imagining a task and then solving it, ALG starts from work that professionals have already completed, such as a studio’s production character, a released circuit board, a researcher’s published simulation or a film’s locked cut, and derives the task backward from it, keeping the finished work as the reference. The synthesizer never has to solve the task. It only has to take finished work apart, so the difficulty of the tasks is set by the work they come from and not by what the synthesizer can do.

ALG spans D professional domains in y of the 13 industry clusters of Agents’ Last Exam. It covers engineering design, from mechanical CAD and CNC machining to electronic design, building information modelling and geospatial analysis; content production, from 3D modelling and animation to game development, film editing and music engraving; and science and medicine, from simulation and scientific visualization to medical image analysis and structural biology. Its tasks run in A professional applications, including Blender, FreeCAD, KiCad, QGIS, 3D Slicer, ParaView, PyMOL, Godot, Shotcut and MuseScore, and they read and write the native project formats of each.

Breadth does not come at the expense of depth. Every task in ALG is a complete professional project rather than an isolated action. It corresponds to a real unit of work, demands hours of expert work and a chain of interdependent decisions, and must deliver a native project file that passes the domain’s own checks, from design-rule checks and re-renders to re-run simulations. Tasks are stated the way such work arrives in practice, as a bug report, a change order or a director’s notes, never as a list of steps.

Our experiments show three things.

  1. The tasks are frontier-hard. Even the strongest frontier agents complete only p% of ALG tasks end to end, against q% on a control set of everyday office tasks built with the same pipeline.
  2. The tasks are as hard as the work they come from. They take experts a median of h hours, and w% of verified tasks defeat the model that built them.
  3. Training on them transfers. Training on ALG improves model by a points on Agents’ Last Exam and transfers to independent domain benchmarks, with gains that keep growing with data.
